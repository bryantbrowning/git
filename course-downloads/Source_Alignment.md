## Week 3 · The Privacy Survival Guide

Unit 3, Lessons 1–2.

- Submit an original graphic organizer identifying at least three privacy risks, why they matter and at least three practical protections.
- Use your organizer to decide which protections are most important and explain why.
- Create one original one-page guide for university students with a creative title, three risks/solutions and meaningful visual elements.
- Include one or two opinion paragraphs, 8–12 sentences total, with a clear position, reasons, evidence/examples, connections and conclusion.
- Identify any outside information sources used in the guide.
- Submit a 3–5-minute video showing the organizer and guide, explaining choices and reasoning, and reading all written paragraphs aloud.

**Worked example:** Think Before You Click: phishing → verify through a trusted channel; reused passwords → unique passwords/password manager and MFA; oversharing → review visibility/permissions. The opinion model prioritizes checking requests and account access.

**Example writing:** 10 sentences in 2 paragraph(s).

**Exact supplied brief:**

> Teach the Document #3: The Privacy Survival Guide
> Your Mission: Protect Your Digital Life!
> Every day, university students use smartphones, social media, online shopping platforms, artificial intelligence, and other digital services. Although these technologies make life more convenient, they may also create risks involving personal information and online privacy.
> Imagine your university has asked you to create a Digital Privacy Survival Guide to help other students protect themselves online.
> Your challenge is to investigate digital privacy risks, decide which protections are most important, and create an original one-page guide that teaches other university students how to stay safe.
> You will complete three parts.
> Part 1 — Brainstorming Graphic Organizer
> Before designing your guide, brainstorm the most important digital privacy risks university students might encounter.
> You may use a mind map, T-chart, comparison chart, cause-and-effect diagram, or another graphic organizer of your choice.
> Your graphic organizer must identify:
> At least three digital privacy risks university students should understand.
> Why each risk is important or potentially harmful.
> At least three practical ways students can protect their privacy.
> Which protections you believe are most important and why.
> Consider topics such as weak passwords, phishing messages, social media oversharing, location tracking, app permissions, AI chat privacy, and public Wi-Fi security.
> You may investigate additional topics that interest you.
> Part 2 — Create Your One-Page Privacy Survival Guide
> Using your brainstorming organizer, design an original one-page survival guide intended for university students.
> Your guide must contain the following:
> A. Creative Title
> Create an interesting title that captures your audience's attention.
> For example: Think Before You Click!, Your Digital Life, Your Rules, or The Student's Guide to Staying Private Online.
> B. Three Privacy Risks and Solutions
> Highlight at least three important privacy risks and provide practical recommendations for addressing them.
> Explain these ideas using graphics, illustrations, icons, diagrams, or other visual elements.
> C. Opinion Writing — 1–2 Paragraphs
> Write one or two well-developed paragraphs (8–12 sentences total) explaining which digital privacy protections you believe are most important for university students.
> Your writing must include:
> A clear opinion or position.
> Supporting reasons explaining your choices.
> Evidence, relevant information, or specific examples.
> Logical organization and connecting expressions.
> A concluding statement reinforcing your opinion.
> You are encouraged to research beyond the textbook and use trustworthy information. Identify any outside sources used in your guide.
> Creative Freedom: You may design your guide as an infographic, poster, illustrated checklist, digital brochure, comic-inspired guide, or another original one-page format.
> Your design should communicate important information clearly and help your audience understand your recommendations.
> Part 3 — Teach the Document Video (3–5 Minutes)
> Record a 3–5-minute video presenting your Privacy Survival Guide.
> Your video must include:
> 1. Introduce Your Guide
> Explain the purpose of your survival guide and why digital privacy is important for university students.
> 2. Explain Your Brainstorming and Choices
> Show your graphic organizer and discuss how you developed your ideas.
> Explain why you selected these particular privacy risks and why you believe your recommended protections are important.
> 3. Present Your Creative Guide
> Show your finished one-page guide. Explain how you organized the information and why you selected your visual elements, examples, and recommendations.
> 4. Read Your Written Paragraph(s)
> Read your complete opinion paragraph or paragraphs aloud clearly.
> 5. Explain Your Reasoning
> Discuss how your research and brainstorming influenced your final opinion. Explain what you hope other students will learn from your guide.
> Your presentation should demonstrate your understanding of the topic and your ability to explain your creative and academic decisions.
> Assignment Submission Requirements
> Submit the following three components:
> Brainstorming Graphic Organizer — your original planning document identifying privacy risks, solutions, and important ideas.
> One-Page Privacy Survival Guide — your creative visual document containing three privacy risks, recommended solutions, and your 1–2 opinion paragraphs.
> 3–5-Minute Video Presentation — explain your choices and reasoning, present your survival guide, and read your written paragraphs aloud.
> Remember: There is no single correct design or opinion. Your goal is to investigate, organize your ideas, develop a supported opinion, and teach your audience something valuable about digital privacy.

## Week 5 · Postcards from Around the World

Unit 4, Lessons 1–2.

- Select a city outside China and investigate authentic monthly temperature and precipitation data.
- Create a four-part seasonal/monthly organizer with temperature, precipitation and important observations.
- Create four original illustrated postcards representing four different months or seasons, supported by a climate graph.
- Write one or two descriptive paragraphs, 8–12 sentences total, explaining the city’s annual climate patterns.
- Record a 3–5-minute video explaining the destination, month choices, designs and climate evidence, and read all written paragraphs aloud.

**Worked example:** Tokyo, Japan: January, April, July and October. Each card combines a creative illustration, monthly mean temperature and precipitation; a full 12-month graph supports the annual pattern.

**Example writing:** 10 sentences in 2 paragraph(s).

**Exact supplied brief:**

> TTD #5 - Postcards from Around the World
> The mission: Imagine you are spending an entire year living in a city outside China. You want to show your friends what the climate is like throughout the year.
> Students select an international destination and investigate its climate using authentic monthly temperature and precipitation data.
> They create four illustrated postcards, each representing a different month or season.
> Each postcard should communicate something meaningful about the location's climate through illustrations, photographs, weather information, and numerical evidence.
> For example, a student investigating Oslo could select January, April, July, and October and explain how conditions typically differ.
> Academic requirements
> Graphic organizer: A four-part seasonal comparison chart with temperature, precipitation, and important observations.
> Writing: One or two descriptive paragraphs (8–12 sentences total) summarizing the city's annual climate patterns.
> Creative document: Four original postcards, supported by a climate graph.
> Video: A 3–5-minute presentation explaining the destination, the choice of months, the postcard designs, the climate information, and reading the paragraphs aloud.
> Students could make their postcards humorous, artistic, nostalgic, realistic, or travel-themed.

## Week 6 · Climate Doppelgängers

Unit 4, Lessons 3–4.

- Choose two cities with at least one data-supported climate similarity; name both cities and countries.
- Research monthly temperature and precipitation and read at least one English-language source about either or both climates.
- Create one original organizer/chart/infographic/data visual with at least one similarity, three meaningful differences/comparisons, four accurate numerical observations across both measures and a short discovery conclusion.
- Use equivalent measures and periods; label °C/mm and identify the data sources.
- Write one or two comparison paragraphs, 8–12 sentences total, including the similarity, three comparisons, four numerical observations, comparative/superlative language, logical connections and a conclusion.
- Submit a 3–5-minute video explaining city choices, investigation, visual design, evidence and reasoning, including reading all paragraphs aloud.
- Submit the investigation visual, comparison writing and video, with sources.

**Worked example:** Tokyo and Hong Kong: both have warm Augusts. Compare January means, June precipitation and the wettest month. All reference values use 1991–2020 normals; Tokyo is a city in Japan and Hong Kong is a city in China.

**Example writing:** 10 sentences in 2 paragraph(s).

**Exact supplied brief:**

> Teach the Document #6 — Climate Doppelgängers
> Your Mission: Two Cities, One Climate Mystery!
> Can two cities appear to have similar climates but actually be very different?
> Imagine you are a climate investigator researching two cities in different parts of the world. At first glance, these cities may appear to have similar temperatures, precipitation levels, or seasonal weather patterns. However, a closer investigation might reveal surprising differences.
> Your challenge is to find two cities that share at least one climate characteristic, investigate their climate data, and explain their most interesting similarities and differences.
> You will complete three parts.
> Part 1 — Climate Investigation: Graphic Organizer or Data Visualizer
> Step 1: Choose your cities
> Select two cities that share at least one climate characteristic.
> For example, you might investigate cities that:
> Have similar annual average temperatures but different seasonal patterns.
> Receive similar amounts of annual precipitation but experience different rainy seasons.
> Have similarly warm summers but very different winters.
> Experience similar temperatures during one month but very different conditions during other months.
> You may choose cities anywhere in the world. International comparisons are encouraged.
> Step 2: Investigate the climate data
> Find reliable climate information about both locations, including monthly temperature and precipitation data.
> Read at least one English-language source explaining climate conditions in one or both locations.
> Step 3: Create your climate investigation visual
> Create one original graphic organizer, comparison chart, infographic, or data visualization presenting the results of your investigation.
> Your visual must include:
> The names and countries of both cities.
> At least one important climate similarity supported by data.
> At least three meaningful climate differences or comparisons.
> At least four accurate numerical observations from your research, covering both temperature and precipitation.
> A short conclusion explaining what makes these cities surprising climate doppelgängers.
> You may use a Venn diagram, comparison chart, annotated climate graph, detective evidence board, or another appropriate visual format.
> Use clearly labeled measurements, such as degrees Celsius (°C) and millimeters (mm). Compare equivalent measurements and time periods, and identify your data sources.
> Part 2 — Climate Comparison Writing (1–2 Paragraphs)
> Using the information from your investigation, write one or two well-developed paragraphs (8–12 sentences total) comparing the climates of your chosen cities.
> Your writing must include:
> A clear topic sentence introducing both locations and the purpose of your comparison.
> At least one significant similarity between the two climates.
> At least three meaningful differences or comparisons.
> At least four accurate numerical observations supporting your descriptions.
> Appropriate comparative and superlative language.
> Logical connecting expressions such as similarly, however, whereas, while, in contrast, and compared with.
> A concluding sentence summarizing your most important discovery.
> Your writing should explain the overall climate patterns rather than simply list individual numbers.
> Remember: Your comparisons must be based on actual climate data rather than personal opinions or assumptions.
> Part 3 — Teach the Document Video (3–5 Minutes)
> Record a 3–5-minute video presentation teaching your audience about your Climate Doppelgängers investigation.
> Your video must include the following:
> 1. Introduce Your Cities
> Introduce both locations and explain why you selected them. What made you think their climates might be similar?
> 2. Explain Your Investigation and Visual
> Show your graphic organizer, chart, or data visualization.
> Explain how you organized your information and why you chose that particular visual format.
> 3. Reveal Your Climate Discoveries
> Explain the similarities and differences you discovered.
> Discuss the numerical evidence supporting your findings and identify anything you found surprising.
> 4. Read Your Written Paragraph(s)
> Read your complete climate comparison paragraph or paragraphs aloud clearly.
> 5. Explain Your Reasoning and Conclusion
> Explain why you selected these particular comparisons and what you want your audience to learn about interpreting climate information.
> Your video should demonstrate your understanding of the climate data and your ability to explain your choices, not simply involve reading your paragraphs aloud.
> Submission Requirements
> Submit all three components:
> 1. Climate Investigation Visual
> One original graphic organizer, comparison chart, annotated graph, infographic, or data visualization containing your climate evidence.
> 2. Climate Comparison Writing
> One or two paragraphs (8–12 sentences total) describing important climate similarities and differences using numerical evidence.
> 3. Video Presentation (3–5 Minutes)
> A recorded presentation explaining your investigation, creative choices, climate evidence, and conclusions, including reading your written paragraphs aloud.
> Include the sources used to obtain your climate information.
> Remember: Your objective is not simply to show that two cities have different weather. Your goal is to discover meaningful climate patterns, communicate them accurately, and explain why your comparisons are interesting.
