# Unlock 2 — Listening & Speaking, Units 2–4

Twelve standalone HTML presentations, each with a continuous 90-minute plan. Speaking is the main classroom output: explanations, questions, decisions, supported reports, listener reconstruction, feedback and a better second attempt. The 258 timed canvases contain 1,080 minutes of planned classroom activity. Optional extensions sit outside those minutes or replace an equal-length stage.

Open index.html after extracting the ZIP. Each lesson also opens independently. These are browser presentations with JavaScript; they are not native PowerPoint files. Classroom documents, images, fonts and scripts are embedded. No website or account is required to present.

The textbook is Unlock, second edition, Level 2 Listening and Speaking, Cambridge University Press. Printed student-book pages 36–79 were checked from the uploaded unit 2-3.pdf (PDF pages 5–48); pages 80–101 were checked from unit 4.pdf (PDF pages 1–22). The first four pages of unit 2-3.pdf contain the book map. OCR supported extraction, and rendered pages were inspected to establish unit/page boundaries and key activities.

The source spine is specific to this Listening & Speaking book. Reading & Writing assignment rules were not copied into this course. The four supplied Unit 1 guides remain preserved byte-for-byte in the working reference folder; the twelve new files cover Units 2–4.

## Use the classroom tools

- Task gives the procedure, output and exact activity minutes. Book enlarges the original source inside the current presentation. Listen provides teacher-read material, a hidden transcript, optional local browser speech and local media playback. Language provides useful moves. TTD contains the brief, source, example, cue planner and checklist. Teacher includes facilitation, source boundaries, keys for original practice and the full timetable.
- Arrow keys or the activity selector navigate the lesson. On cue activities, Next reveals the next cue before moving on. B opens Book, T opens TTD and N opens Teacher. Escape closes book enlargement or restores the main task view. Focus and Full screen support projection.
- The timer can time a speaking turn or a whole activity. It never advances the lesson automatically. Group work is concurrent; a ten-minute group-of-four performance allows four 2-minute contributions and four 30-second responses, not ten minutes of speech per learner.
- Use printed or personal A/B cards for a private information gap. The projector’s neutral view is safe to share. When everyone sees one source, frame the activity as a needs-based negotiation.
- Optional voice recording stays in the open page. Download each take before closing. Browser/microphone support varies; the speaking activities do not depend on the recorder.
- Cue notes and TTD checkboxes are shared between each lesson pair in the same browser. Export editable paired cues for backup or transfer. Imports reject other pairs and the separate Reading & Writing format. Exporting is the dependable way to move work between devices; local-file browser storage behavior can vary.

## Audio and source boundaries

Publisher recordings were not supplied as a verified playable collection. Every lesson has a complete original teacher-read input, its own questions and its own key. Original fictional cases are labelled; the late Unit 4 explanations use the supplied textbook topic and state their source limits. Optional browser speech uses an available local English voice. If unavailable, read the Teacher script or choose your own licensed local media file. A local file remains on the current canvas and is not uploaded.

The publisher route is also specified per lesson. Use the actual publisher recording and the book’s questions together; original practice answers do not fill the publisher’s audio/video gaps. No official publisher answer key is claimed. Live event prices, timetable details and regional climate forecasts require current sources rather than an old learning document.

## Teach the Document cycle

Six assignments: each launches in Lesson 1 or 3 and is due at the start of the following lesson. Each final submission is one 3–5-minute English explanation of a readable genuine document, from short cue notes. Learners teach 3–5 terms and 3–5 key ideas with evidence, ask at least two real comprehension questions, give a supported evaluation/limitation and summarize with a source credit. The instructor announces the actual calendar and collection route.

Odd lessons protect minutes 78–88 for the launch and paired clinic. Even lessons collect/check at 0–3, sample short teaching moments at 3–7 and transfer improvements at 82–86. The class does not watch every full recording in those short blocks. Worked examples are short demonstration extracts, clearly labelled; the 4-minute route supports a full 3–5-minute submission. Students explain their own ideas rather than reading the model script.

## Feedback and assessment

Use a suggested 20-point teacher rubric for each TTD: source/evidence accuracy 4; understandable explanation and useful vocabulary 4; organization and cue-based delivery 4; comprehension checks and listener response 4; supported evaluation, limits and credit 4. Score each area separately:4 clear/accurate/complete;3 mostly effective;2 partial;1 minimal;0 absent. Completion checkboxes are not quality scores. Do not use automated fluency or accent scoring.

Use located feedback: “When you said ___, I understood ___.” Then “Improve ___ by ___.” The replay must contain a specific improvement. Offer frames, fewer points and a cue check for support; use an alternative audience, fair objection or source-limit question for challenge. Judge intelligibility and source accuracy rather than speed or accent conformity.

## Scientific care in Unit 4

Weather preferences and small classroom surveys describe a group; they do not establish causal effects on learning. One local afternoon is not a climate trend. Intonation can suggest mood but is interpreted with words, context and speaker differences. Cause and effect require a connecting mechanism, not merely sequence. Distinguish melting land ice and warming seawater, which contribute to sea-level rise, from floating sea-ice loss and its habitat/hunting effects. The text’s undated regional projections are presented as textbook claims, not current forecasts. Current numerical and local claims require dated evidence.

### Original listening input and key

This is original teacher practice, separate from publisher media.

Host: This is a fictional preview of Riverside Winter Weekend, not the real Harbin festival. The event takes place in the town park on Saturday. There are three main activities. In the morning, an artist teaches visitors how to make small ice sculptures. You need to book this workshop because there are only twelve places. At lunchtime, local families sell warm food near the entrance. In the evening, visitors can follow a trail of lights beside the river. The trail is outside, so bring warm clothes. The sculpture workshop costs eight pounds; the food is sold separately. The light trail is free. Visitor: I enjoy making things, but I cannot arrive before lunch. Host: Then the workshop will not work for you. You could try the food and walk along the light trail later. Please check the weather before coming; the plan may change if the path is unsafe.

**Gist:** A plan for choosing activities at a fictional winter event

1. Where and when is the event? **Answer:** Town park; Saturday.
2. Which activity needs a booking, and why? **Answer:** Morning sculpture workshop; only twelve places.
3. What can the visitor do after arriving at lunchtime? **Answer:** Try the food and visit the evening light trail.
4. Which detail is still uncertain? **Answer:** Whether the path/weather will allow the planned activities; check before coming.

### Detailed facilitation


**00–03 · Follow the festival.**

Explain a festival clearly from short listening notes, then recommend one activity.

- Read the speaking mission. Tell a partner what a listener should be able to understand by the end.
- Locate Task, Book, Listen, Language, TTD and Teacher on this canvas.
- TTD 1 launches at 78 minutes and is due at the next lesson’s start.

Keep: Explain a festival clearly from short listening notes, then recommend one activity.

Teacher: Set pairs and a group-of-four route before beginning. Use the embedded teacher-read input if publisher media are unavailable. No account, installation or live website is needed.


**03–08 · Start with a real listener.**

What makes an event worth travelling to: the activity, the people, or the place? Give one example and ask why your partner chose differently.

- Think for 30 seconds; choose one specific example.
- A speaks 45 seconds; B asks one why/how follow-up for 30 seconds. Swap.
- Use the remaining two minutes to compare answers with another pair and keep one useful contrast.

Keep: Two answers, reasons and relevant follow-ups.

Teacher: Do not force personal disclosures. Students can discuss a plausible third-person case. Listen for a reason, not only a preference.


**08–12 · Words you can explain.**

Explain useful words in simple English before using them in a speaking task.

- Choose three of the six cards; try a definition before revealing it.
- Partner gives an example or asks a clarification question.
- Reveal the explanation and repair meaning if necessary.

Keep: Three simple definitions and examples.

Teacher: Accept paraphrases and examples. Do not spend the block copying dictionary definitions. Cards are classroom explanations, not automatic vocabulary scoring.


**12–15 · Describe it. Don’t name it.**

A describes a word without saying it; B identifies it and uses it in a relevant sentence.

- 30 seconds for each clue. Rotate roles after each word.
- Try four rounds in two minutes.
- Use the last minute to improve an unclear clue.

Keep: Four oral clues with context.

Teacher: Use the next-word control. Give a gesture or example as support; stretch by asking for a contrast or non-example.


**15–18 · Observe. Predict. Check.**

Use the Harbin photos on p.38. Say one thing you can observe and one prediction about visitors. What evidence would confirm your prediction?

- Spend 45 seconds looking at the Book/source image.
- A gives an observation; B gives a prediction and a reason. Swap.
- Keep one question to check during listening.

Keep: One observation, one prediction and one check question.

Teacher: Do not treat a prediction as a correct answer before listening. Book video questions require the actual publisher video; do not use a practice script to mark them.


**18–22 · Listen for the whole idea.**

Listen once without a transcript. Choose the main idea, then justify it to a partner.

- Teacher reads the complete original practice script at a natural clear pace, or plays a prepared local recording.
- Choose silently, then explain which part supports your choice.
- Teacher checks the main idea after pairs have spoken.

Keep: A gist choice with a reason.

Teacher: Default route: original practice input, with its own questions/keys. If using licensed publisher media instead, select the specified textbook questions and book notes; do not transfer this practice key to the book. The same block length applies.


**22–27 · Listen again. Keep useful detail.**

Listen again. Keep short answers to the four practice questions, then compare.

- Read the four questions for 30 seconds.
- Listen a second time; write keywords, not full sentences.
- A and B compare for 90 seconds and mark one uncertainty. Check using Teacher after discussing.

Keep: Four short answers and an uncertainty check.

Teacher: The original script is around one minute at a clear pace. Give a second reading if needed within the five-minute block. Keep the transcript hidden until the detail discussion is complete.


**27–31 · Make the notes smaller.**

Turn the information into three brief cue cards. Keep the relationship, not every word.

- For one minute, select the important words from your notes.
- Compare your choices with a partner for one minute.
- Reveal the sample cues, then explain one item the sample leaves out and why.

Keep: Three cue cards; each should prompt speech rather than replace it.

Teacher: Short cues differ between learners. These sample cues are one option; retain accurate alternative notes. Ask the listener which detail they still need.


**31–35 · Your words. Their reconstruction.**

Explain the listening from short cues. Your partner reconstructs the message from listening alone.

- A:60-second retell. B:30 seconds to reconstruct the main point and ask a question.
- Swap for another 90 seconds.
- Use the final minute to repair one missing or inaccurate detail.

Keep: Two retellings, two reconstructions and one repair.

Teacher: Hide the full script. If learners are ready, hide one cue after the first attempt. Check source accuracy before fluency.


**35–40 · Choose the useful form.**

Choose a response, explain why it works, then use the same pattern with a different idea.

- Discuss both choices before checking the explanation.
- Each learner makes one original example.
- Partner asks one question that makes the speaker use the pattern again.

Keep: Two checked choices and two original oral examples.

Teacher: The choices have source-specific explanations. A correct click is not the speaking outcome: require a reason and a new use of the pattern.


**40–45 · Repair the message.**

Make each statement more accurate, useful or appropriate for its listener.

- Try both repairs orally before revealing the sample.
- A explains the first problem; B explains the second.
- Choose one new situation and make a careful version together.

Keep: Two repairs with an explanation of what improved.

Teacher: A sample is not the only acceptable wording. Preserve accurate alternative answers. For scientific/source claims, accuracy takes priority over a neat sentence frame.


**45–51 · Ask for the missing information.**

Use separate A/B cards. Ask, clarify and check before recommending.

- 1 minute: distribute printed cards or read privately on individual devices. Keep the projector on the neutral view.
- 2 minutes: A explains; B asks at least three relevant questions.
- 2 minutes: swap communication jobs. 1 minute: check unresolved details.

Keep: Questions, checked information and an unresolved detail.

Teacher: For a genuine information gap, distribute the private cards separately. If everyone sees the same source, use this as a needs-based negotiation. Never claim that shared projector cards are private.


**51–55 · Make a reasoned choice.**

Agree on one feasible activity for the visitor. Explain why the attractive morning workshop does not fit. Name the missing route/weather information.

- Each partner proposes an option with a reason for 45 seconds.
- Use the evidence, audience and limit controls to explain what matters most.
- Agree on a decision and one condition or next check.

Keep: A decision, supporting evidence and a qualification.

Teacher: The weight controls support discussion; they do not calculate an objective correct decision. A smaller number of important points can outweigh many weak points.


**55–59 · Let the listener hear the point.**

Sentence stress · make the important detail audible.

- Notice the model chunks. Teacher models; learners echo once.
- A says a chunk; B reconstructs the key word/meaning. Swap.
- Use the feature in one new explanation, not only a repeated sentence.

Keep: Two intelligible new sentences and listener confirmation.

Teacher: Use teacher voice or the optional local English browser voice. Browser voices vary and are not publisher audio or pronunciation assessment. Focus on intelligibility; avoid treating accent difference as an error.


**59–63 · Notice how the model works.**

Listen to a short model, then identify its purpose, evidence and limit.

- Hide the model text for the first reading.
- Listen; partner states the opening position and one supporting detail.
- Reveal the text and identify one move to use in your own turn.

Keep: A structure map and one borrowed speaking move.

Teacher: The model is a short worked example, not a complete two-minute student performance. Discuss its structure; do not require word-for-word memorization.


**63–66 · Four cues. A clear route.**

Prepare brief cues for the speaking task. Recommend one activity from the fictional event to your partner. Include when/where, two accurate details, one reason linked to the visitor’s needs, and one check before booking.

- 1 minute: opening/main point and the audience.
- 1 minute: two source-linked supporting details.
- 1 minute: limit, listener question and ending. Set groups/roles before performance.

Keep: Four brief cue boxes, ready for speech.

Teacher: Differentiate with two main points and a frame. Stretch by adding a fair alternative and a source limit. Protect the following performance block from extra planning.


**66–73 · Take the floor. Give it back.**

Recommend one activity from the fictional event to your partner. Include when/where, two accurate details, one reason linked to the visitor’s needs, and one check before booking.

- 1 minute: silent cue check and listener-job check.
- A speaks 2 minutes; B asks a question and A responds within 30 seconds.
- Swap for 2 minutes 30 seconds. Use the final minute for one evidence check.

Keep: Record the recommendation, one supporting detail and one unanswered question.

Teacher: Seven minutes covers both two-minute turns, two 30-second responses and setup/checking. It is not seven minutes of speaking per learner. Keep optional recording local and learner-initiated.


**73–78 · Keep. Change. Say it again.**

Use your listener’s evidence to improve one section.

- 1 minute: give one keep/change comment each.
- A gives a 60-second improved section; B gives a 30-second response. Swap.
- Use the final minute to name the audible improvement.

Keep: A specific comment and an improved second attempt.

Teacher: Use: “At the moment when you said ___, I understood/did not understand ___.” Avoid vague “speak better/faster” comments. Replays should change content/organization/language meaningfully.


**78–82 · TTD 1 · Festival guide: teach the choices**

Launch your next 3–5-minute English Teach the Document recording, due at the next lesson’s start.

- 1 minute: read the complete brief and source-selection criteria in the TTD panel.
- 2 minutes: inspect the worked source and short teaching demonstration.
- 1 minute: choose a source direction and explain why it fits.

Keep: A source choice, purpose and teaching direction.

Teacher: Four minutes introduces the assignment; the paired clinic continues on the next canvas. The worked model is a short demonstration extract, not the full required recording. Assessed speech uses cue notes, not full-script reading.


**82–86 · Teach one useful idea.**

Use the worked source, or your chosen readable source, to rehearse a short teaching segment.

- 30 seconds: choose one term and one source detail.
- A teaches for 60 seconds and asks one real question; B responds for 30 seconds. Swap.
- Final 30 seconds: keep one useful improvement.

Keep: Two mini-teaching turns and genuine comprehension checks.

Teacher: Keep the document visible. This is a rehearsal sample, not a replacement for the final 3–5-minute recording. If a source is unreadable or unsuitable, use the provided worked example for practice and select another before recording.


**86–88 · Leave with a teachable plan.**

Use the TTD checklist and four-minute route to prepare the next step.

- Check source credit,3–5 useful terms/ideas, evidence, two questions, evaluation and summary.
- Say the next action and confirm the next-lesson deadline.
- Export the paired cue notes if changing devices.

Keep: A clear next action and saved TTD plan.

Teacher: The checklist shows completeness, not automatic quality or a grade. Teacher checks accurate evidence and explanation. Allow a reasonable alternative recording route if the optional in-browser recorder is unavailable.


**88–90 · Say the change. Keep the cues.**

Tell your partner one improvement you made and one next step. Export cue notes before closing if you need a backup.

- A and B each give a 30-second spoken exit.
- Use the last minute to save/export and check the next task.

Keep: A spoken improvement, next step and usable backup.

Teacher: Keep the exit inside two minutes. Optional extension tasks sit outside the 90 minutes or replace a named stage of equal duration.

### Original listening input and key

This is original teacher practice, separate from publisher media.

Reporter: We are at a fictional Community Arts Day. What time does the event start? Organizer: It usually starts at ten. Today we are opening at eleven because the artists need extra time. Reporter: What are people doing in the main hall now? Organizer: They are putting up paintings. The visitors are waiting in the café. Reporter: Do children need a ticket? Organizer: Children under twelve enter free with an adult, but everyone needs to book a workshop place. Reporter: Where do the workshops take place? Organizer: In the small classroom behind the hall. Today we are offering drawing and dance. Reporter: Is food included? Organizer: No. You can buy food in the café or bring your own lunch. Reporter: Does the event finish at five? Organizer: Usually, yes. Today the last dance workshop is finishing at six. Please read today’s programme rather than an old advertisement.

**Gist:** Questions that clarify a changing festival programme

1. What is different about today’s opening time? **Answer:** Usually ten; today eleven.
2. What are people doing now in the hall? **Answer:** Putting up paintings.
3. Who needs a workshop booking? **Answer:** Everyone, including children who enter free.
4. Why is today’s programme important? **Answer:** Today’s times differ: opening eleven; last workshop finishes six.

This is a fictional campus radio report. On Monday morning, students could not open the online timetable. The problem was not a broken ocean cable. A local network device had lost power. 4. What limitation does the backup have? **Answer:** An old copy can be wrong when rooms change; check the date.

This is a fictional classroom exercise, not a real research study. A teacher asked eight volunteers to learn six new words. 2. What different activities did the groups do? **Answer:** Examples/self-testing versus saving a phone word list.
3. What two outcomes are described? **Answer:** First group recalled more words; second found the list faster in this fictional activity.
4. Why can we not generalize? **Answer:** Tiny groups, different methods and a fictional classroom exercise, not real research.

Today I am reporting on a fictional study-group video service called StudyLink. My argument is that it may help a small group practise speaking when they cannot meet in person. ### Original listening input and key

This is original teacher practice, separate from publisher media.

This is an original model explanation, not publisher audio. Our presentation explains one pathway from burning fossil fuels to coastal risk. Burning coal, oil and gas adds carbon dioxide to the atmosphere. Additional greenhouse gases make it harder for heat to escape to space, so the Earth warms. Warming causes seawater to expand and land ice to melt. As a result, global sea level rises. Higher sea levels can increase flooding risk for low-lying coastal places. Local risk also depends on the land, storms, tides and protection. Melting floating sea ice is a different pathway: it affects habitat and hunting conditions, but it is not the main direct explanation for rising sea level. We are not using an old textbook projection as a current forecast. To discuss a particular town, we would need current local information. In conclusion, a clear climate presentation explains each link and distinguishes a general mechanism from a local prediction.

**Gist:** An explanation links emissions to coastal risk with limits

1. What begins this pathway? **Answer:** Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide/greenhouse gases.
2. What two processes raise sea level here? **Answer:** Warming seawater expands; melting land ice adds water.
3. What affects local flood risk besides global sea level? **Answer:** Land, storms, tides and protection.
4. What source would a town-specific forecast need? **Answer:** Current local information and dated evidence.
